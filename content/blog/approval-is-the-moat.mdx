Most of the AI-vendor conversation right now is a race to remove humans from
the loop. Autonomous agents. Zero-touch automation. Set-and-forget workflows.
The pitch is that the value is in the removal — every human touchpoint is a
cost, and the vendor's job is to reduce that cost to zero.

Nebbos is built on the opposite bet: the human in the loop is the moat.

## Why the removal is fake

The removal is fake in two ways.

First — inside any consequential enterprise decision, there is a named human
who owns the outcome. The controller who signed off on the journal. The head
of people who approved the offer. The compliance officer who attested to
the filing. That accountability does not go away because a vendor's marketing
copy says "autonomous." It goes underground. When the decision blows up, the
CFO is still asked who signed off, and if the honest answer is "the AI did
it without a human sign-off," the audit finding writes itself.

Second — the removal is fake for training. Every preference pair your team
generates — every human accept, every edit, every reject with a reason — is
the highest-signal training data your organization will ever produce. A
vendor pitching "zero-touch" is pitching zero training signal. The
autonomous version of your workflow is the version that stops learning.

## Approval as substrate

The Nebbos design is to make the human sign-off scale, not to remove it.

That means an approval graph as a first-class substrate primitive. Every
action a Pearl proposes routes through the graph — configurable per
department, per action class, per risk tier, with delegation chains for when
the primary approver is unavailable. A close-week reconciliation goes to the
controller. A journal above threshold goes to the controller plus the CFO. A
public-communication draft goes to the department head plus legal. The
graph is versioned, auditable, and inspectable — the auditor can see who
should have approved what, whether they did, and how long it took.

The graph does more than route. It compresses the sign-off. Instead of a
controller reviewing every reconciliation from scratch, the Pearl surfaces
the reconciliations that differ from baseline plus the specific mechanical
details. Instead of a compliance officer chasing evidence across systems,
the Pearl assembles the evidence packet and the officer's job becomes the
last-mile judgment call. The human's attention is spent on the
consequential decision, not on the workflow that surrounds it.

## Why this is the moat

Two reasons.

First — every approval is training data your team owns. Over twenty-four
months of running, that accumulates into a preference-pair corpus that
captures your organization's specific judgment on your specific work. A
competitor cannot buy that. A vendor cannot bundle that into a release. It
belongs to you, and it moves with you if you ever leave the platform. That
is the twenty-three-percent inference-of-revenue moat the AI-native
benchmark actually rewards.

Second — every approval is regulatory-adequate evidence. The EU AI Act
Article 14 requires human oversight of high-risk AI systems. SOX requires
attested human sign-off on material controls. HIPAA requires accountable
persons on privacy-relevant actions. Every regulator that will matter over
the next decade is converging on the same answer: the human in the loop is
the accountability. A vendor stack that removed the human has to reinstate
one under duress. A vendor stack that made the human the substrate is
already ready.

## The counter-argument, honestly

The counter-argument is speed. The vendor pitching autonomous will say — and
they are not entirely wrong — that the human in the loop is friction, and
friction slows the enterprise down. The Nebbos answer is not to deny the
friction. It is to compress it. A well-designed approval graph reduces the
human's time on a routine action from minutes to seconds, without removing
the human. That is a very different problem from removing the human, and it
is the problem Nebbos was built around from Layer 11 up.

## The claim, made plainly

Autonomous AI systems will exist. Some of them will be quietly useful for
low-stakes decisions. None of them will run the consequential operations of
serious enterprises for very long, because the accountability model does not
survive contact with the first regulator investigation, the first blown
audit, the first material variance nobody can name a signer on.

The company brain that persists is the one built around the human in the
loop, not the one designed to remove them. Approval is the moat.
